ASL Alphabet Classifier

Spell a Word

Recognized Letters:

Project Statement: This process required our group members to gather original data by modeling the ASL alphabet to program the machine to recognize each letter. The goal was for the machine to easily identify the letter that the user portrays to the camera. We aimed to gather enough data, usually 300–400 pictures per letter, so that the machine had a large variety of examples in the database to assist in differentiating the signs. Throughout this process, each group member contributed to collecting images of different letters, allowing us to build a larger and more diverse data set. We initially expected that once the machine was trained, it would be able to recognize the different hand gestures with a relatively high level of accuracy. When first testing the Teachable Machine, we quickly realized that not enough data was collected for each letter. This led to an unreliable machine that could not easily identify the ASL signs. It was critical for us to capture more data and make note of the lighting, background, and other elements that could interfere with the capturing of the data. We recognized that even small changes in the positioning of our hands or the angle of the camera could impact the machine's ability to accurately identify the letters. One example was ensuring that we had data that gave both left and right handed people representation in the system. This demonstrated the importance of collecting a diverse range of images rather than repeatedly capturing the same hand gesture from one position. After ensuring that each sign had over 300 photos, the system demonstrated more coherence but continued to struggle to differentiate signs that appear similar. ASL letters such as "A", "E", and "S" are very similar with slight differences, and the system failed to consistently identify those differences. The same setback occurred with "M", "N", and "T", which have similar hand positioning but represent completely different letters. Although the machine became more reliable as we increased the amount of training data, it was clear that the quantity of images did not necessarily guarantee accurate results. This process demonstrated how important the quality and variety of training data are when developing a machine learning system. While the Teachable Machine was able to recognize several ASL letters, its limitations highlighted how difficult it can be for AI to identify subtle differences that humans can more easily recognize. This experience helped our group better understand the challenges of training AI models and the importance of continuously testing and improving their performance. Once those lessons were clear, our main method for improving the accuracy was more pictures. The more pictures we have with as many different angles, distances, camera quality, lighting, the more accurate the AI can get. We decided to attempt to just about double the sample size we used in the previous iteration of the machine and see if that helped in deciphering letters with similar hand gestures. That is where we have gotten in our most recent version of the machine. The current iteration of the machine uses over 700 pictures for each letter in order to get as close to accurate as we can for as much of the alphabet as possible. It is able to differentiate certain letters it could not before, but is still lacking in consistency. Once we got it to the current state, we decided to build on to it a bit more, instead of just reading sign language from the camera, it can now spell things out. Referencing the p5js website, we figured out how to use millis to record the most frequently recorded letter in a 2 second time frame and then display that letter. Every 2 seconds it adds the most frequent letter withing that time frame until whatever the user intends to spell is listed. The main drawback of this feature is the camera and spelling section span across a good amount of screen space. Because of this, in it's current form, the spelling feature may only be available to computers or those with large enough screens on their devices. One lesson from "Unmasking AI", is that projects such as this should be accessible to people other than just the person who created it. In order for us to make our machine as accessible as possible, we made sure our data included signs for both the left and right hands. Our data also contains photos of varying camera qualities, which could assist in making sure the machine recognizes hand signs from people without access to top tier technology. Another lesson from Buolamwini is how innacurate AI systems can have a negative impact on people and those systems should always be questioned. If an AI with the same design and accuracy as ours were to be used in a more important context, such as translating for someone who needs it, it would not be able to give results that a person could rely on. Since we are aware our machine cannot, in it's current state, give 100% accurate results, we have made sure to explicitly state how and why that is the case here. To conclude, the lessons we have learned from 'Unmasking AI' have really resonated with us because it becomes aparent how AI systems can become biased based on why designs it and whose experiences are considered during development. Although our team has diverse representation, it is not enough to call this an unbiased system. This machie learning project is evidence that the data that goes into teaching a machine matters. Three people and their hands are not enough to make this system accurate enough to apply in real world scenarios. Our project demonstrated that even when developers have good intentions, implicit biases can influence the decisions they make and the overall performance of their systems. Furthermore, the concept of intersectionality helped us undertsand that accesibility cannot be measured by how well the system works for one individual. Different identites and technological resources can impact how a user experiences the AI system. We learned that it is important to consider these differences throughout the entire development process rather than trying to fix it after the final design is made public and problems may arise.
