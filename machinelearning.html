ASL Alphabet Classifier

Project Statement: This process required our group members to gather original data by modeling the ASL alphabet to program the machine to recognize each letter. The goal was for the machine to easily identify the letter that the user portrays to the camera. We aimed to gather enough data, usually 300–400 pictures per letter, so that the machine had a large variety of examples in the database to assist in differentiating the signs. Throughout this process, each group member contributed to collecting images of different letters, allowing us to build a larger and more diverse data set. We initially expected that once the machine was trained, it would be able to recognize the different hand gestures with a relatively high level of accuracy. When first testing the Teachable Machine, we quickly realized that not enough data was collected for each letter. This led to an unreliable machine that could not easily identify the ASL signs. It was critical for us to capture more data and make note of the lighting, background, and other elements that could interfere with the capturing of the data. We recognized that even small changes in the positioning of our hands or the angle of the camera could impact the machine's ability to accurately identify the letters. One example was ensuring that we had data that gave both left and right handed people representation in the system. This demonstrated the importance of collecting a diverse range of images rather than repeatedly capturing the same hand gesture from one position. After ensuring that each sign had over 300 photos, the system demonstrated more coherence but continued to struggle to differentiate signs that appear similar. ASL letters such as "A", "E", and "S" are very similar with slight differences, and the system failed to consistently identify those differences. The same setback occurred with "M", "N", and "T", which have similar hand positioning but represent completely different letters. Although the machine became more reliable as we increased the amount of training data, it was clear that the quantity of images did not necessarily guarantee accurate results. This process demonstrated how important the quality and variety of training data are when developing a machine learning system. While the Teachable Machine was able to recognize several ASL letters, its limitations highlighted how difficult it can be for AI to identify subtle differences that humans can more easily recognize. This experience helped our group better understand the challenges of training AI models and the importance of continuously testing and improving their performance.
